# CLSC: A Counterfactual Causal Inference Workflow for Longitudinal Compositional Microbiome Data

# ABSRACT

Longitudinal microbiome data, inherently high-dimensional, sparse, short, noisy, and compositional, defy conventional causal inference frameworks, with Mendelian randomization hobbled by a scarcity of valid instrumental variables and gnotobiotic animal trials rendered impractical for large-scale use by prohibitive costs. To overcome these limitations, we developed a counterfactual causal inference framework for longitudinal compositional microbiome data by coupling a hierarchical extended Kalman filter with a negative-binomial observation equation to an exactly-convex synthetic control over the inferred latent log-ratio dynamics, with the former directly accommodating zero counts and dropouts of sparse taxa without imputation. The framework was systematically evaluated through comprehensive simulation studies covering eleven data-generating scenarios, including time-series length and sampling regularity, noise, zero inflation, natural succession, and effect size, as well as through ablation, sensitivity, placebo-permutation, and bootstrap inference, and the results indicate that it can recover the sign and magnitude of intervention effects with reasonable accuracy, control the Type I error rate, and remain robust to data perturbations. The framework shows promise for individual-level causal inference in the simulations and empirical analyses presented here and may offer a useful analytical tool for host-microbiome causal research.

Keywords: microbiome causal inference; compositional data analysis; synthetic control; EKF-RTS; longitudinal microbiome; counterfactual analysis.

# INTRODUCTION

Advances in microbiome science, fueled by the widespread adoption of high-throughput sequencing technologies and the accumulation of longitudinal multi-omics cohorts, have shifted the field from descriptive taxonomic profiling toward functional mechanistic dissection, with researchers no longer merely characterizing community composition but striving to elucidate how microbes regulate host health and how interventions causally reshape microbial communities (Wang et al., 2026). However, microbiome data are inherently compositional, high-dimensional, and sparse, while longitudinal observations are often limited by short sampling periods and sparse time points, making conventional statistical and causal inference methods difficult to apply; moreover, most current studies remain descriptive, reporting correlations and taxon co-occurrence patterns rather than disentangling true causal signals, confounding effects, and intrinsic temporal drift, which remains a key bottleneck for translating microbiome findings into precision medicine (Khelfaoui et al., 2025, 2026).

To meet the demand for causal inference, researchers have employed a range of analytical tools, yet each has its own strengths and limitations (Ascandari et al., 2026; Jiang and Li, 2026). Although Mendelian randomization has been widely adopted in epidemiology to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes using genetic variants as instrumental variables, valid instrumental variables remain extremely scarce in microbiome research, where microbial abundance is shaped by thousands of host genetic loci and environmental factors, making it difficult to identify genetic variants that simultaneously satisfy the assumptions of strong relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction (Liu et al., 2022; Taba et al., 2025). Randomized controlled trials based on germ-free animal models and fecal microbiota transplantation are likewise regarded as the gold standard for causal identification, yet their high costs and ethical constraints restrict them to a small set of cultivable species or specific strain combinations and prevent them from recapitulating the complex, highly individualized microbial ecological networks in humans (Gu et al., 2025; Guggeis et al., 2025, 2025; Wong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). A more pervasive difficulty is that conventional differential abundance tests merely compare group-level mean changes before and after intervention, without accounting for individual-specific temporal trends, and thus readily misclassify natural microbial succession as an intervention effect (Lyu et al., 2023; Sankaran and Jeganathan, 2024; Li et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025). Despite their success in constructing counterfactuals in econometrics, standard synthetic control methods, when applied directly to microbiome count data, ignore the zero-inflated nature of count matrices and violate Aitchison geometry, causing donor weights to deviate from true compositional proportions and thereby producing severe estimation bias (Gloor et al., 2017; Ha et al., 2020; Degli Esposti et al., 2021).

To systematically address these challenges, we propose a quasi-experimental causal inference framework rooted in the counterfactual paradigm, hereafter referred to as Compositional Longitudinal Synthetic Control (CLSC). The framework takes as input a three-dimensional count tensor  where indexes taxa, indexes time, and indexes donors or subjects; in R, corresponds to a three-dimensional array. Rather than operating on the three axes simultaneously, CLSC proceeds through three coupled stages that act along different dimensions: compositional observation modeling along the axis, hierarchical extended Kalman-RTS smoothing along the axis, and exactly-convex synthetic control and counterfactual estimation along the axis (Gerber, 2015). First, in the preprocessing and compositional observation modeling stage, taxa are filtered by detection rate, while sparse sequencing counts are retained in their raw form and directly modeled through a negative-binomial observation equation, with the latent state represented in centered log-ratio coordinates; zero counts are treated as genuine realizations of count variability rather than imputed values, thereby avoiding the strong distributional assumptions of Bayesian multiplicative zero replacement while preserving Aitchison distance and eliminating the spurious correlations inherent in compositional data (Palarea-Albaladejo and Martín-Fernández, 2015; Díaz, 2019). Second, the latent-state denoising stage employs a hierarchical shared-parameter extended Kalman filter with an RTS smoother, whose nonlinear negative-binomial observation equation directly captures dropout and overdispersion, extracting denoised latent-state trajectories from short, noisy time series while overcoming parameter non-identifiability caused by the limited number of time points per subject (Newman et al., 2023). Third, on the donor axis, in the synthetic control and counterfactual estimation stage, an exactly convex weighting problem with non-negative weights summing to one is solved by projected gradient over the donor pool, so that the counterfactual always lies in the convex hull of donor trajectories without any ad hoc weight renormalization; a counterfactual control unit whose trajectory closely matches the treated unit's pre-intervention profile is then synthesized from a large pool of untreated donors, enabling individual-level causal effect estimation without extrapolation (Ben-Michael, Feller and Rothstein, 2022).

To systematically evaluate the statistical performance and robustness of CLSC, we first assessed its statistical power and Type I error control through comprehensive simulations and compared it with the unsmoothed synthetic control method; we then examined its practical applicability in a real longitudinal microbiome cohort. By combining placebo permutation, FDR control, and bootstrap confidence intervals, the framework can identify individualized causal effects and further reveal the corresponding biological effects (Chernozhukov, Wüthrich and Zhu, 2021).

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, we propose CLSC, a modular counterfactual causal inference framework that simultaneously addresses the compositional constraints, temporal noise, and individual-level heterogeneity inherent in longitudinal microbiome data. Second, we establish a simulation-driven evaluation protocol that, through benchmarking, ablation, sensitivity, and robustness analyses, quantitatively disentangles the independent and synergistic contributions of compositional count observation modeling and hierarchical extended Kalman temporal smoothing, thereby providing evidence for the statistical design of causal microbiome studies. Third, we provide an open-source R implementation of the proposed framework and reproducible simulation vignettes to support individual-level causal inference in host-microbiome research.

Ablation analyses were performed to isolate the contributions of compositional preprocessing and temporal smoothing. The geometry ablation re-ran the full pipeline on the eleven effect datasets under all combinations of zero replacement (multiplicative, Bayesian, none) and transformation (none, CLR, rCLR, ALR, ILR). ## Benchmark Against Alternative Estimators

CLSC achieved the smallest mean magnitude error (0.156 CLR units) across the eleven effect datasets, slightly outperforming the unsmoothed synthetic control (SC, 0.159), CLR-scale difference-in-differences (DiD, 0.160), the linear mixed model (LMM, 0.160), and the Wilcoxon test (0.160), whereas the raw-count edgeR baseline yielded a magnitude error as high as 0.995 and recovered effect signs only for a subset of taxa (Figure 4A, D). All CLR-based methods achieved complete detection (power = 1.00) with Type I error rates close to the nominal level (0.025 for CLSC and approximately 0.000 for the other CLR-based methods), whereas edgeR achieved a power of only 0.11 with a Type I error rate of 0.015 (Figure 4B). The noise sweep further showed that, as the innovation standard deviation increased from 0.2 to 2.0, CLSC and unsmoothed SC were nearly indistinguishable in point-estimation accuracy; at low to moderate noise, DiD was slightly more accurate than CLSC, but at the highest noise level (innovation SD = 2.0), CLSC degraded less than DiD (0.603 vs. 0.656), indicating that EKF-RTS smoothing helps suppress accumulated innovation noise under high noise (Figure 4C). Overall, CLSC offers no substantial advantage over CLR-scale baselines in point-estimation accuracy, but its strengths lie in a decisive improvement over raw-count methods and in the structural guarantees of an individual-level, exactly-convex counterfactual with placebo/FDR inference and a well-defined pre-fit diagnostic.

## Empirical Application and Biological Interpretation

We applied CLSC to publicly available longitudinal microbiome or multi-omics cohorts, including DIABIMMUNE, TEDDY, and HMP/iHMP, to assess its practical applicability. An exogenous intervention or natural event was defined as the intervention node. Individuals with complete pre- and post-intervention series formed the treated units, and contemporaneous untreated individuals with overlapping sampling schedules formed the donor pool. The per-subject counterfactuals closely tracked the pre-intervention trajectories, indicating good pre-period fit (Figure 6A). Using placebo permutation with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control across taxa, complemented by subject-level bootstrap intervals, CLSC identified individualized causal effects on microbial composition (Figure 6B, C). These estimates can be further interpreted to reveal biological effects, although detailed functional validation remains beyond the scope of the present framework. Overall, the empirical application supports the utility of CLSC for individual-level causal inference from real longitudinal microbiome data and illustrates how such estimates may inform biological interpretation.

# DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed the CLSC framework for sparse, short longitudinal observational microbiome data with individual-specific spontaneous succession. The core motivation of this framework is that conventional causal inference methods are difficult to adapt directly to the compositional, high-dimensional, sparse, and short-time-series characteristics of microbiome data, while existing alternative strategies each suffer from limitations such as scarcity of instrumental variables, high cost, or overly strong statistical assumptions (Corander, Hanage and Pensar, 2022). By integrating compositional count observation modeling, hierarchical extended Kalman temporal denoising, and exactly-convex synthetic control, CLSC provides a feasible and relatively robust analytical path for individual-level causal inference of microbial dynamics. Unlike traditional differential abundance tests that merely compare group-level mean differences, CLSC explicitly constructs individualized counterfactual trajectories, thereby helping to distinguish intervention-induced microbial changes from spontaneous temporal fluctuations and inter-individual heterogeneity.

Simulation results showed that, compared with the unsmoothed raw synthetic control, CLSC controlled the Type I error rate and relatively robustly recovered the sign and magnitude of intervention effects across scenarios covering spontaneous succession, high technical noise, zero inflation, irregular sampling, and different effect sizes. These results suggest that, in longitudinal microbiome causal inference, respecting compositional geometry and individualized temporal structure has practical significance for avoiding systematic bias. Ablation experiments further revealed the relative contributions of each module: compositional preprocessing, especially zero replacement combined with the CLR transformation, is crucial for estimation accuracy. Running the estimator on raw counts without the Aitchison transformation severely distorts effect magnitudes, while omitting zero replacement in zero-inflated data increases the effect estimation error by approximately fifty percent. This finding is consistent with the basic theory of compositional data analysis, namely that ignoring the closure constraint and zero-inflated structure leads to spurious correlations and weights deviating from true compositional proportions. In contrast, temporal smoothing mainly stabilizes counterfactual diagnostics, such as pre-RMSE and its signal-to-noise ratio, without substantially biasing the point estimates themselves. Therefore, EKF-RTS smoothing plays more the role of a diagnostic regularizer in the present framework than that of an accuracy-critical step. Among the weighting estimators, the exactly-convex simplex has relatively good robustness and interpretability, because it always yields feasible weights within the convex hull of donor states, avoiding weight extrapolation and post hoc normalization that may arise in penalized weight estimation. Hyperparameter sensitivity and robustness analyses further indicated that CLSC maintains relatively stable inference across different zero-inflation levels, irregular sampling schedules, noise magnitudes, and donor-pool sizes, although estimation accuracy decreases under extreme zero inflation and high noise, which is consistent with the reduced information content of the data.

This study still has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results and generalizing the method. First, CLSC assumes that intervention effects act only on latent microbial states, and the current framework does not model time-varying effect profiles or the biological mechanisms underlying heterogeneous individual responses. This means that when intervention effects change over time or individual response patterns differ substantially, the framework may only capture average or cumulative effects, rather than resolving dynamic response processes. Future work could consider introducing time-varying effect parameters or hierarchical effect models to more flexibly characterize the evolution of post-intervention microbial trajectories. Second, the exactly-convex counterfactual requires a sufficiently diverse donor pool; when the donor pool is small, or when baseline microbial profiles differ substantially between donors and treated units, the quality of counterfactual reconstruction declines. This limitation is shared by synthetic control methods and reflects the dependence of counterfactual estimation on donor information coverage. In practical applications, pre-fit diagnostics should be used to assess counterfactual reliability, and effect estimates should be interpreted cautiously when the donor pool is insufficient. Third, the model assumes independence across taxa and ignores inherent microbial co-abundance associations. Species interactions and co-abundance networks are ubiquitous in microbial communities, and ignoring these associations may lead to oversimplification of individual taxon effects. Future work could incorporate multivariate state-space structures that account for species interactions, or adopt a hierarchical Bayesian framework to model between-taxon covariance. Fourth, BH-FDR control is applied simultaneously to all taxa; in the simulations presented here, this still detected all six affected taxa in each scenario (power 6/6) and controlled Type I error, but in very large taxon sets of hundreds to thousands, the multiplicity burden increases, and restricting inference to a pre-specified candidate set may become preferable. In addition, bootstrap confidence intervals are centered on the point estimate rather than on the true latent effect, so they quantify the sampling uncertainty of the estimator; the observed truth coverage was low and should be interpreted as a reproducibility check rather than as exact calibration intervals. Finally, empirical validation is based only on public longitudinal cohorts, and external independent validation cohorts are still lacking; further testing across different populations, intervention types, and sequencing platforms is needed to evaluate the generalizability of CLSC.

Despite these limitations, CLSC provides a practical tool for individual-level causal inference in non-randomized longitudinal microbiome studies. It helps separate intervention-induced microbial changes from spontaneous temporal fluctuations and reduces confounding bias common in conventional cross-sectional analyses. Compared with methods that rely on strong instrumental variables or costly animal experiments, CLSC is more applicable to observational longitudinal cohorts and can output individualized counterfactual trajectories and effect estimates, providing testable hypotheses for subsequent mechanistic studies. Although this paper demonstrates the framework using microbiota responses to vaccination as an example, it can potentially be extended to longitudinal microbiome studies of other exposures and host phenotypes, such as dietary interventions, antibiotic exposure, infection events, or immunotherapy. Future work could further combine external validation, multi-center cohorts, and more flexible effect models to improve the applicability and reliability of CLSC in real-world causal microbiome research.

The denoised latent trajectories from hierarchical latent temporal smoothing serve as the input to the synthetic control module. Because the constraint is imposed during optimization, the reconstructed counterfactual always lies in the closed convex hull of the donor latent states, and no ad hoc post hoc weight renormalization is required.

The donor weights are always exactly convex, and no penalized donor-weight estimator is used. Compared with conventional synthetic control, the exactly-convex weights restrict the counterfactual to the convex set spanned by donor latent states and prevent extrapolation to biologically implausible microbial states. All taxa enter the analysis, and multiplicity is controlled by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure on the full taxon set. The reliability of a counterfactual is summarized by the pre-period fit quality: the per-coordinate pre-period root mean squared error of the constructed counterfactual against the treated unit's own pre-intervention trajectory, and the signal-to-noise ratio ; effects with a poor pre-period fit are treated as unreliable (Abadie-style diagnostic). Effect estimates, weights, and counterfactuals carry the subject and taxon labels of the input, so estimates can be read directly per microbiota (taxon name).

Statistical significance of the estimated effects is assessed by a placebo (permutation) procedure. The estimator is re-run with every donor unit acting as a pseudo-treated unit (the remaining donors forming the pseudo-pool), yielding a placebo distribution of effect sizes per taxon. Because fits are performed per coordinate, each taxon is then evaluated by a two-sample permutation test comparing the treated units' effects on that taxon with the donor placebo effects; the resulting -values are corrected across taxa with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. Uncertainty of the mean effects is additionally quantified by subject-level bootstrap (resampling units with replacement to give percentile confidence intervals).

## Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Quantitative Evaluation Metrics

Hyperparameter sensitivity is assessed by re-running the full pipeline over the detection-rate threshold (default 0.1); the zero-handling and transformation combinations (multiplicative + CLR (default), multiplicative + rCLR, none + rCLR); and the donor-pool size (default 30). These grids cover the preprocessing hyperparameters, whereas data-level perturbations are covered by the scenario family itself.

Quantitative evaluation metrics are defined as follows. Type I error is the fraction of the 50 taxa declared significant (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR ) under the null scenarios. Statistical power is the fraction of the six effect-bearing taxa declared significant () under the effect scenarios; both are evaluated within the single cohort of a mode because the placebo test builds the null distribution within the same cohort. Estimation quality is summarized by the sign correctness of the six estimated effects, the bias and mean absolute error of the point estimates against the ground-truth added effect (in CLR units), the pre-intervention counterfactual fit and its signal-to-noise ratio , and the feasibility of the donor weights (sum of weights for the simplex estimator). In addition, subject-level bootstrap percentile intervals () on the six effect taxa of and quantify interval width, median bootstrap standard error, and, as a calibration diagnostic, coverage of the true effect.

## Software Implementation

The CLSC framework is implemented in the open-source R package clsc, which provides a modular and reproducible workflow covering compositional count observation modeling, hierarchical extended Kalman temporal smoothing, exactly-convex (and penalized) synthetic control, causal effect estimation, pre-fit quality diagnostics, and placebo/bootstrap inference. The package includes the simulation engine underlying all experimental scenarios, evaluation utilities for Type I error, statistical power, and counterfactual reconstruction error, and a vignette reproducing the simulation study reported in this paper. The package is publicly available on GitHub and released under a permissive open-source license.
